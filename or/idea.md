This video from Artem Kirsanov explores a groundbreaking study published in Nature Neuroscience (January 2025) regarding the physical "hardware" limitations of the human brain that dictate what we can and cannot learn.

Core Concept: Neural "Rivers" and Channels
The video explains that neural activity flows through the brain much like water through a landscape. Just as a river is guided by established channels, our brain activity has preferred pathways shaped by physical wiring and circuitry [01:33].

The Experiment: Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCI)
To test whether the brain is infinitely flexible, researchers used monkeys and BCIs to directly monitor and manipulate neural patterns:

Dimensionality: Researchers recorded 90 neurons in the motor cortex. Each neuron's firing rate creates a coordinate, forming a trajectory in a "90-dimensional space" [09:38].

The Projection: Since we can't see 90 dimensions, researchers "projected" this data into 2D paths on a screen (like a shadow) [10:21].

The Discovery: When moving a cursor from point A to B, the brain uses a specific sequence. However, when moving from B back to A, the brain does not simply play the sequence in reverse. It uses an entirely different set of neural patterns [12:31].

The "Hidden" Limits
The study attempted to force the monkeys to learn "impossible" patterns:

The Constraint Task: Monkeys were rewarded only if they could move the cursor within a narrow corridor that required them to reverse a previous neural sequence [13:54].

The Result: Despite clear visual feedback and strong motivation (rewards), the monkeys consistently failed to reverse the natural flow of their neural activity [14:35].

Key Takeaways
Intrinsic Dynamics: Certain patterns of brain activity are physically impossible to achieve because they go against the brain's "intrinsic dynamics" [15:22].

Learning Difficulty: Struggles in mastering a new skill may not be due to a lack of practice or motivation, but rather because the skill requires neural sequences that conflict with your brain's biological architecture [15:15].

Biological Reality: The brain is not an infinitely programmable computer; it is a biological system with fixed rules and preferences [15:45].

## 1. Executive summary

Centerstone by Glenride is a SaaS platform that helps organizations improve learning and performance outcomes by identifying friction in skill development and dynamically adapting training flow, feedback, and interface structure.

Rather than assuming every learner can succeed through the same fixed path, Centerstone uses behavioral telemetry and outcome data to detect where individuals and cohorts hesitate, loop, disengage, or plateau. It then recommends or applies more effective learning pathways.

The product is designed for organizations with high-value, high-complexity training environments where slow ramp time, repeated mistakes, and learner drop-off create operational and financial cost.

---

## 2. Problem statement

Most training systems are designed as static curricula. They assume:

- all learners follow the same progression,
- more repetition solves most failure,
- interface friction is secondary to learner effort,
- and training outcomes can be judged only at the end.

In reality:

- some task transitions are predictably harder than others,
- the order in which material is introduced matters,
- certain feedback styles help one learner but hinder another,
- and many performance failures are caused by training design rather than motivation.

This creates measurable business pain:
- increased time-to-proficiency,
- lower completion rates,
- greater support burden,
- higher error rates in live environments,
- and reduced confidence among learners.

# 2. Founder memo

## Founder memo: Why we’re building Centerstone by Glenride

Most organizations still treat learning failure as a motivation problem.

If someone struggles, the standard answer is predictable: repeat the module, assign more practice, add a manager, add more content. But in complex environments, that explanation is often incomplete. People do not struggle randomly. They tend to struggle in patterns. Certain transitions create hesitation. Some workflows trigger repeated correction loops. Some interfaces overload learners before they can build fluency. And some forms of feedback help one learner progress while causing another to stall.

We believe an important category is emerging around this problem: software that does not just deliver training, but continuously adapts it based on how people actually perform.

That is why we are building Centerstone by Glenride.

Centerstone is based on a simple idea: before asking people to push harder, we should ask whether the path itself can be improved. Instead of assuming every learner should move through the same sequence at the same pace with the same prompts, we capture evidence from performance and use it to identify where friction occurs and what changes produce better outcomes.

This matters because training is expensive, but preventable failure is even more expensive. In healthcare, technical operations, industrial environments, and enterprise software onboarding, the cost of slow proficiency compounds quickly. It shows up in support burden, poor confidence, delayed readiness, higher error rates, and lower retention.

We are not building speculative neurotechnology. We are not trying to diagnose people or make claims about fixed ability. We are building a grounded, enterprise-ready platform that turns training telemetry into practical adaptation.

Our belief is that the next generation of training software will not be static. It will be responsive. It will observe, diagnose, and adapt. It will help organizations understand not only whether learners are failing, but where they are failing, why they are failing, and which changes actually improve outcomes.

Centerstone is our first step toward that future.

In the near term, we are focused on high-value training environments where small improvements in proficiency have outsized operational value. Over time, we see a larger opportunity: becoming the adaptive layer for human performance across software environments more broadly.

The long-term vision is not just better training. It is better alignment between the way systems are designed and the way people actually learn, decide, and perform.

That is the future we want to build.

— **Glenride Founding Team**

## Executive summary

Centerstone by Glenride is a B2B SaaS platform that helps organizations improve training and performance outcomes by identifying where learners struggle and adapting the path forward. The product focuses on learning friction — the moments where people hesitate, repeat mistakes, loop, overload, or disengage within a training workflow.

Most training systems are static. They deliver content, track completion, and report outcomes after the fact. Centerstone introduces an adaptive layer that helps organizations detect bottlenecks in real time or near-real time, test interventions, and improve progression based on observed performance signals.

The company’s initial wedge is high-value training environments where slow proficiency and repeated remediation carry significant cost. These include simulation-based healthcare training, technical onboarding, industrial certification, and software enablement.

Centerstone’s long-term goal is to become the adaptive infrastructure layer for human performance in software — beginning with training, and eventually expanding into broader workflow optimization.

## Problem

Organizations invest heavily in training, onboarding, and workforce readiness. Despite this, many teams still struggle with:

- long ramp times,
- poor visibility into learner bottlenecks,
- repeated learner errors,
- inconsistent proficiency across cohorts,
- high remediation burden,
- static training paths that do not adapt.

The core failure in many systems is not lack of content, but lack of adaptation. Teams often cannot answer basic operational questions with confidence:

- Where exactly are learners getting stuck?
- Which steps create repeated friction?
- What intervention would help?
- Which changes improved outcomes?
- Are issues concentrated in one cohort, module, or workflow transition?

Without this visibility, training design remains reactive and inefficient.

# 5. Objection handling guide

## Objection: “We already have an LMS.”
**Response:**
That makes sense. Centerstone is not designed to replace your LMS. It complements your existing system by adding friction analysis, adaptive interventions, and workflow-level visibility that most LMS platforms do not provide.

---

## Objection: “We already track completion and assessment scores.”
**Response:**
That’s a strong starting point. The gap we usually see is between outcome reporting and intervention insight. Completion and scores show what happened. Centerstone helps show where friction started and what changes might improve the path.

---

## Objection: “This sounds like a lot of integration work.”
**Response:**
That’s exactly why we start with a narrow pilot. We scope one workflow, define a minimum telemetry model, and keep the first deployment lightweight so the customer can validate value before broader rollout.

---

## Objection: “We’re not looking for AI hype.”
**Response:**
We agree. Centerstone is designed to be evidence-first and practical. The product is focused on measurable performance signals and interpretable interventions, not black-box claims.

---

## Objection: “We don’t have enough learner volume.”
**Response:**
That depends on the workflow and the signal quality. In some cases, even modest volume can reveal obvious friction patterns, especially where task structure is consistent. We’d evaluate that during pilot scoping.

---

## Objection: “We’re worried about learner privacy.”
**Response:**
That’s an important concern. Centerstone is designed with role-based access, configurable retention, and governance controls. We also position the product as performance support, not diagnosis.

---

## Objection: “We already know where the problem is.”
**Response:**
That may be true, and if so, Centerstone can still help by making the problem measurable, tracking whether interventions work, and helping you compare outcomes over time and across cohorts.
